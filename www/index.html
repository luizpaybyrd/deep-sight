How this simulation works

Everything you see is computed from spectra on a 400–700 nm grid at 5 nm resolution. No color filters, no guessing.

1. Light entering the water

The sky is modelled as a Planck blackbody at the colour temperature of the condition you pick (bright sun ≈ 5800 K, overcast ≈ 7000 K, dusk ≈ 3100 K, moonlight ≈ 4100 K), scaled by realistic relative intensity — moonlight is roughly a millionth of noon sun.

2. The water column

Each water type is built from three real optical constituents, not a paint colour:

  • Pure water absorption a_w(λ) — Pope & Fry values. This is why red dies first.
  • CDOM / tannin — a_g(λ) = G·e^(−0.0165(λ−440)). Crushes blue, leaves red. Tea-stained lakes.
  • Chlorophyll — absorption peaks at 440 and 675 nm, leaving a green window.
  • Suspended sediment — strong scattering b(λ) plus mineral absorption. Mud kills contrast, not just colour.

Downwelling light attenuates as E(λ,d) = E₀(λ)·e^(−K_d(λ)·d). The horizontal path from lure to eye uses beam attenuation c = a + b, and lost radiance is replaced by veiling light — the backscattered glow that washes a lure into the background in muddy water.

3. Lure reflectance

Each colour is a hand-authored reflectance spectrum, not an RGB triple — so "red" genuinely reflects only above 590 nm and genuinely turns black at depth. Fluorescent colours additionally absorb short-wavelength energy and re-emit it in their own band, which is why they hold visibility deeper than their non-fluorescent equivalents.

4. The fish's eye

Cone sensitivities use the Govardovskii A1 visual-pigment template at each species' measured λmax:

  • Snoek (northern pike) — 460 / 530 / 615 nm. Trichromat, so blue still reaches it.
  • Snoekbaars (zander) — dichromat, 533 & 613 nm, plus a tapetum lucidum. The turbid-water, low-light specialist of the Dutch canals.
  • Baars (European perch) — 460 / 537 / 617 nm. The sharpest daylight colour vision in the canal.
  • Roofblei (asp) — 455 / 535 / 620 nm.
  • Karper and blankvoorn — cyprinids with a fourth, ultraviolet cone near 365 nm. That channel is real and this screen cannot render it.
  • Meerval (wels catfish) — 540 & 615 nm over a rod-dominated retina. It hunts by barbel and lateral line.
  • Paling (European eel) — effectively monochromatic, one pigment near 523 nm. Brightness only.
  • Largemouth bass — dichromat, 535 & 614 nm. Nearly blind to blue.
  • Walleye — 533 & 605 nm. Rainbow trout — 434 / 531 / 576 nm. Bluegill — 455 / 535 / 625 nm.

Two water presets are tuned for the Netherlands: Polder canal (moderate tannin, a real algae load, some suspended silt — the summer default) and Winter canal, when the algae dies back and the same water becomes dramatically clearer. Switching between them with the same lure is the single most instructive thing on this page.

Dichromat views are rendered on the orange↔teal axis their two cones actually span. Below scotopic threshold the rod pigment takes over and the image collapses to monochrome — which is exactly when silhouette beats colour.

5. Sun, sky and surface

Light is not a preset. The sun's elevation comes from the NOAA solar-position equations for your date, clock time and latitude (Central European time, DST approximated to the usual late-March→late-October window). Utrecht on 21 June resolves to sunrise 05:18 and sunset 22:03; on 21 December, 08:46 and 16:29 — both within a minute of the true times.

The sky spectrum is then built from that elevation: Kasten–Young air mass, Rayleigh optical depth τ_R = 0.008735·λ⁻⁴·⁰⁸ and an Ångström aerosol term. The reddening at sunset and the collapse in intensity both fall out of the physics — neither is drawn in by hand. Cloud cover shifts direct light into diffuse and attenuates the total.

Two surface effects matter more than anglers expect. Fresnel reflection: with the sun low, much of the light bounces off the water rather than entering it — transmission falls from ~96% at noon to under 80% near the horizon. And the refracted beam runs at a slant, so it covers more water per metre of depth, which raises the effective K_d. Below the horizon the model crosses over to twilight and then to a moonlight floor set by the moon-phase control.

6. How far away it can be seen

Contrast alone doesn't decide detection — angular size does too. The lure subtends α = 2·atan(L/2r), and the contrast threshold climbs as α approaches the eye's minimum resolvable angle: ε = ε₀·(1 + (4·MAR/α)²), with ε₀ rising as the rods take over. Detection range is where that index crosses 1.

This is why lure size buys you very little in a turbid canal — there, range is capped by contrast, not resolution — and a great deal in clear water. It is also why the meerval and the paling, with far coarser acuity, see the same lure at roughly half the range a perch does.

7. The predator's edge — why dusk

The day strip carries a second, green trace. It is not light: it is the ratio of how far your fish can see the lure to how far a blankvoorn can, computed every half hour. The shaded bands are where that ratio peaks.

Nothing about this is folklore — it falls straight out of the eye models. A zander's tapetum lucidum and low rod threshold keep it seeing after a roach has effectively gone blind, so the ratio climbs as the light collapses and falls back to ~1.0 at midday when both see perfectly well. In a polder canal in September that puts the peak near 06:00 and 20:30, at about 8×. Run the same numbers for a roofblei and you get 1.8×; for a rainbow trout, no window at all. Those are daylight hunters, and the model says so without being told.

Change the date and the windows move with the Dutch daylight — 08:00 and 17:00 in late December, 04:00 and 22:30 in June.

8. Local forage

The forage group holds what a Dutch predator actually eats — blankvoorn, alver, baarsje, pos and the now-ubiquitous zwartbekgrondel. The match figure is the distance between your lure and the nearest of them in the selected fish's own cone space, under the current conditions. High means your lure reads as food; low means it reads as an intruder. Both catch fish; they are different arguments, and it helps to know which one you are making.

9. Visibility score

Weber contrast in cone-excitation space (achromatic) combined with chromatic separation in the fish's own opponent channel, gated by whether there are enough photons for the species to detect anything at all.
